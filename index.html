The Surface Ocean CO₂ Mapping (SOCOM) Program

SOCOM is a community-driven initiative that benchmarks the statistical and machine-learning methods used to reconstruct global surface ocean CO₂ fugacity (sfCO₂) and estimate air–sea CO₂ fluxes (FCO₂). Building on the first phase of SOCOM, the second phase (SOCOMv2) uses coordinated experiments to evaluate these reconstruction methods under sparse observational constraints and to assess how reconstruction biases propagate into FCO₂, providing a transparent framework for more accurate and reliable assessments of the global ocean carbon sink.

About

What is SOCOM?

The ocean plays a central role in the global carbon cycle, taking up about 30% of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions and helping to regulate Earth's climate. Quantifying this ocean carbon sink requires robust global estimates of the air–sea CO₂ flux (FCO₂), the net exchange of CO₂ between the atmosphere and the ocean.

FCO₂ is driven by the difference in CO₂ between the surface ocean and the atmosphere, and can therefore be estimated from measurements of surface ocean CO₂ fugacity (sfCO₂), compiled in the Surface Ocean CO₂ Atlas (SOCAT). Because these measurements do not cover the whole ocean, statistical and machine-learning sfCO₂ reconstruction methods fill the gaps using variables that influence sfCO₂ and are available everywhere, such as temperature, salinity, and chlorophyll-a from satellite and reanalysis products. The resulting complete global fields, hereafter referred to as sfCO₂ reconstructions, are then used to estimate FCO₂, yielding flux data products.

A major challenge is the sparse and uneven distribution of sfCO₂ observations. Since 1990, SOCAT observations cover only about 2% of the global ocean grid cells at 1° resolution, with particularly large gaps in the wintertime Southern Ocean and the North Pacific. These observational gaps limit the skill of sfCO₂ reconstruction methods and contribute to uncertainties in FCO₂ estimates.

The Global Carbon Budget (GCB) estimates the ocean carbon sink using two complementary approaches: flux data products and global ocean biogeochemical models (GOBMs). It reports persistent discrepancies between the two, particularly in their mean state, spatial patterns, and long-term trends. A key scientific question is to what extent biases in sfCO₂ reconstructions under present-day sampling contribute to these discrepancies.

Initiated in 2013, the Surface Ocean CO₂ Mapping program (SOCOM) brings together the international community developing sfCO₂ reconstruction methods. Its first phase compared 14 sfCO₂ reconstruction methods applied to SOCAT observations, focusing on their estimates of the interannual variability of the ocean carbon sink (Rödenbeck et al., 2015).

To assess how sparse sampling and methodological choices affect sfCO₂ reconstructions, SOCOM launched its second phase (SOCOMv2) in 2023. Through coordinated experiments, SOCOMv2 evaluates sfCO₂ reconstruction methods under sparse observational constraints and assesses how reconstruction biases propagate into FCO₂, supporting more robust estimates of the global ocean carbon sink..

Ocean carbon sink

The ocean absorbs 30% of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions, playing a crucial role in regulating Earth's climate.

Sparse in situ observations

Surface ocean CO₂ observations are sparse and unevenly distributed. sfCO₂ reconstruction methods are therefore needed to estimate conditions in unsampled regions.

Model-observation divergence

Observation-based CO₂ flux products and ocean biogeochemical models show persistent differences in carbon sink mean state and long-term trends.
