The Surface Ocean CO₂ Mapping (SOCOM) Program

SOCOM is a community-driven initiative that benchmarks observation-based mapping methods for reconstructing global surface ocean CO₂ fugacity (sfCO₂) and estimating air–sea CO₂ fluxes (FCO₂). Building on the first SOCOM phase, the second phase (hereafter SOCOMv2) uses coordinated experiments to quantify how observational coverage and methodological choices shape these estimates, providing a transparent framework for more accurate and reliable assessments of the global ocean carbon sink.

About

What is SOCOM?

The ocean plays an important role in the global carbon cycle, absorbing about 30% of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions and helping to regulate Earth's climate. Quantifying this ocean carbon sink requires robust estimates of the FCO₂ globally.

Observation-based reconstructions of surface ocean CO₂ fugacity (sfCO₂) provide a key approach for estimating these fluxes. They combine in situ measurements from the Surface Ocean CO₂ Atlas (SOCAT) with environmental information from satellite observations, and reanalysis products to generate continuous global maps of surface ocean CO₂. These reconstructed sfCO₂ fields are then used to estimate air–sea CO₂ fluxes.

Established in 2013, the Surface Ocean CO₂ mapping intercomparison program (SOCOM) brings together the international community developing these sfCO₂ reconstructions. SOCOM provides a community-driven framework for evaluating, benchmarking, and improving sfCO₂ reconstructions, with the goal of improving confidence in reconstructed sfCO₂ fields and the resulting estimates of air–sea CO₂ fluxes.

A major challenge is the sparse and uneven distribution of surface ocean CO₂ observations. Although SOCAT contains millions of measurements, large regions of the global ocean remain poorly sampled, particularly at high latitudes and during winter. These observational gaps limit reconstruction skill and contribute to uncertainties in estimated air–sea CO₂ exchange.

Recent assessments, including the global carbon budget (GCB), reveal persistent differences between fluxes estimated from these observation-based reconstructions and ocean biogeochemical models, particularly in their mean state, temporal trends, and regional patterns. A key scientific question is the extent to which these discrepancies arise from the sparse and uneven distribution of surface ocean CO₂ observations and the sfCO₂ reconstruct used to reconstruct global sfCO₂ fields.

To better understand these uncertainties, SOCOMv2 initiative, launched in 2023, conducts coordinated experiments to evaluate sfCO₂ reconstructions. By comparing multiple mapping approaches within a common evaluation framework, SOCOMv2 quantifies reconstruction uncertainties and supports the development of more robust estimates of the global ocean carbon sink.

Ocean carbon sink

Sparse in situ observations

Surface ocean CO₂ observations are sparse and unevenly distributed. sfCO₂ reconstruction methods are therefore needed to estimate conditions in unsampled regions.

Model-observation divergence

Observation-based CO₂ flux products and ocean biogeochemical models show persistent differences in carbon sink mean state and long-term trends.
