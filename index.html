The Surface Ocean CO₂ Mapping (SOCOM) Program

SOCOM is a community-driven initiative that benchmarks observation-based mapping methods for reconstructing global surface ocean CO₂ fugacity (sfCO₂) and estimating air–sea CO₂ fluxes (FCO₂). Building on the first SOCOM phase, the second phase (hereafter SOCOMv2) uses coordinated experiments to quantify how observational coverage and methodological choices shape these estimates, providing a transparent framework for more accurate and reliable assessments of the global ocean carbon sink.

About

What is SOCOM?

The ocean plays a central role in the global carbon cycle, taking up about 30% of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions and helping to regulate Earth's climate. Quantifying this ocean carbon sink requires robust global estimates of the air–sea CO₂ flux (FCO₂), this is the net exchange of CO₂ between the atmosphere and the ocean.

Observation-based estimates of FCO₂ rely on surface ocean CO₂ fugacity (sfCO₂) measurements compiled in the Surface Ocean CO₂ Atlas (SOCAT). To fill the gaps between these measurements, statistical and machine-learning sfCO₂ reconstruction methods combine them with environmental information from ocean observations, satellite products, and reanalysis products. The resulting complete global fields, hereafter referred to as sfCO₂ reconstructions, are then used to estimate FCO₂, yielding flux data products.

A major challenge is the sparse and uneven distribution of sfCO₂ observations. Since 1990, SOCAT observations cover only about 2% of the global ocean grid cells at 1° and monthly resolution, with particularly large gaps in the wintertime Southern Ocean and the North Pacific. These observational gaps limit the skill of sfCO₂ reconstruction methods and contribute to uncertainties in FCO₂ estimates.

The Global Carbon Budget (GCB) estimates the ocean carbon sink using two complementary approaches: flux data products and global ocean biogeochemical models (GOBMs) based estimates. It reports persistent discrepancies between the two, particularly in their mean state, spatial patterns, and long-term trends. A key scientific question is to what extent these discrepancies arise from sparse sfCO₂ sampling and from the sfCO₂ reconstruction methods themselves.

Initiated in 2013, the Surface Ocean CO₂ Mapping program (SOCOM) brings together the international community developing sfCO₂ reconstruction methods. Through coordinated intercomparisons, SOCOM evaluates and improves these methods to increase confidence in sfCO₂ reconstructions and the resulting flux data products. Its first phase compared 14 sfCO₂ reconstruction methods applied to SOCAT observations, focusing on their estimates of the interannual variability of the ocean carbon sink (Rödenbeck et al., 2015).

To address the key question above, SOCOM launched its second phase (SOCOMv2) in 2023. SOCOMv2 conducts coordinated experiments to evaluate sfCO₂ reconstructions and the resulting FCO₂ under present-day observational constraints. By comparing multiple sfCO₂ reconstruction methods within a common evaluation framework, SOCOMv2 quantifies reconstruction biases and supports more robust estimates of the global ocean carbon sink.

Ocean carbon sink

The ocean absorbs 30% of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions, playing a crucial role in regulating Earth's climate.

Sparse in situ observations

Surface ocean CO₂ observations are sparse and unevenly distributed. sfCO₂ reconstruction methods are therefore needed to estimate conditions in unsampled regions.

Model-observation divergence

Observation-based CO₂ flux products and ocean biogeochemical models show persistent differences in carbon sink mean state and long-term trends.
