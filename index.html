About

What is SOCOM?

The ocean is a major component of the global carbon cycle, currently absorbing nearly one-third of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions and thereby mitigating climate change. Quantifying the magnitude, variability, and long-term evolution of this ocean carbon sink requires robust estimates of air–sea CO₂ exchange

Observation-based reconstructions of surface ocean CO₂ fugacity (sfCO₂) provide a key approach for estimating air–sea CO₂ fluxes. These reconstructions combine sparse in situ sfCO₂ observations compiled within the Surface Ocean CO₂ Atlas (SOCAT) with environmental predictors derived from ocean observations, satellite products, and reanalyses to generate spatiotemporally complete global sfCO₂ fields. Combined with additional environmental parameters, these fields are used to derive observation-based estimates of air–sea CO₂ fluxes.

The Surface Ocean CO₂ Mapping Intercomparison Project (SOCOM) was established in 2013 to bring together the international community developing these reconstruction approaches. SOCOM provides a coordinated framework for the intercomparison and evaluation of observation-based sfCO₂ reconstructions, with the objective of assessing reconstruction skill and quantifying uncertainties in reconstructed sfCO₂ fields and derived air–sea CO₂ flux estimates.

A major challenge arises from the sparse and heterogeneous distribution of surface ocean CO₂ observations. Although SOCAT contains millions of measurements, observational coverage remains limited across large regions of the global ocean, particularly in high-latitude and seasonally undersampled regions. These observational constraints can substantially influence reconstruction performance and the resulting estimates of air–sea CO₂ exchange.

Recent Global Carbon Budget (GCB) assessments have highlighted persistent differences between observation-based CO₂ flux products and Global Ocean Biogeochemistry Models, particularly in their long-term trends. The extent to which these discrepancies arise from observational sampling limitations and sfCO₂ reconstruction uncertainties remains an open scientific question.

To address this challenge, SOCOM entered its second phase, SOCOMv2, in 2023. Through coordinated multi-model testbed experiments, SOCOMv2 evaluates reconstruction skill under realistic observational constraints and quantifies how reconstruction uncertainties propagate into estimates of air–sea CO₂ fluxes.

Ocean carbon sink

The ocean absorbs nearly 30% of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions, playing a crucial role in regulating Earth's climate.

Sparse observations

Surface ocean CO₂ observations are sparse and unevenly distributed. Reconstruction methods are therefore needed to estimate conditions in unsampled regions.

Model-data divergence

Observation-based CO₂ flux products and ocean biogeochemical models show persistent differences in carbon sink mean state and long-term trends.
